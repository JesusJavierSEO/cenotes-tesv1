Route guide

The Yucatan
Cenote Route

The full run from the Homun cenote ring to the underground rivers of Tulum — with prices, distances and what each zone is actually like.

By Cenotes Homún·Updated August 2026·9 min read

The Yucatan Peninsula holds a geological quirk that exists nowhere else on earth: more than 10,000 freshwater cenotes scattered across the region, all created 66 million years ago by the same meteorite that ended the dinosaurs. This guide maps the full route — from the quietest cenotes in Yucatan state to the most spectacular in Quintana Roo — with what you need to plan it.

Why Yucatan has so many cenotes

Sixty-six million years ago a six-mile-wide meteorite hit what is now the Yucatan coast, creating the Chicxulub crater. The impact fractured the limestone shelf underneath the peninsula, and over time groundwater carved those fractures into thousands of caves and caverns. When a cavern roof collapses, a cenote is born. The result is the densest concentration of cenotes on the planet.

That history matters for your trip, because the cenotes closest to the crater rim look different from the ones further out — and that's what shapes the route below.

Zone 1 — The cenote ring: Homun and Cuzama

The cenote ring is the most underrated part of the route. This circular band, more than 60 miles across, traces the crater rim and holds the highest density of accessible cenotes in the peninsula. No lines, no prices inflated by mass tourism, and water in colors that photos don't quite reach.

For travelers, the center of the ring is Homun and Cuzama, 45 and 50 minutes from Merida respectively.

Homun — 20+ cenotes within 3 miles

Homun is the most cenote-dense town in Yucatan. Its three main parks offer genuinely different experiences — underground, open, semi-open, with a colonial hacienda, with a zip-line, with ATVs — so you can build the exact kind of day you want.

Cuzama — the horse-cart cenotes

Cuzama's three underground cenotes are reached by a small cart pulled by a horse along the rails of an old henequen plantation. It costs roughly $15–20 USD including the ride. The descents are by steep wooden ladders, so it's not for everyone, but nothing else on the peninsula works like it.

Zone 2 — Valladolid: the famous ones

Two hours east of Merida, Valladolid is home to the cenotes that show up in every ranking: Ik Kil, the circular open cenote with vines dropping to the water, and the Dzitnup pair (Xkekén and Samulá), both underground with a single beam of light hitting the surface.

Entry runs about $8–10 USD each. The trade-off is crowds — Ik Kil sits next to Chichén Itzá and absorbs its tour-bus traffic. Go at opening or late afternoon.

Zone 3 — Riviera Maya: Playa del Carmen and Tulum

The cenotes here are a different animal. Instead of the open, emerald-green pools of the ring, the Riviera Maya sits on the longest underwater cave system in the world, so its cenotes are mostly underground and a deeper turquoise blue.

Cenotes Casa Tortuga near Tulum is the most efficient stop: five cenotes of different types in one park, from $45 USD. Gran Cenote is the easiest to visit independently at about $30 USD, and Dos Ojos is the destination for cave divers, part of a documented system running more than 50 miles.

Planning by how many days you have

  • 1 day: Homun from Merida. Two or three cenotes, lunch in the village, back by evening.
  • 3 days: Day 1 Homun, day 2 Valladolid plus Chichén Itzá, day 3 Tulum cenotes. Doable but tight.
  • 5–7 days: The full route without rushing — Merida as a base for the ring, then move east to Valladolid, then the coast, ending in Cancun or Tulum.
⭐ How we'd sequence it

Go west to east: start with the quiet cenotes of the ring while they still feel like a discovery, then work toward the famous ones. Doing it in reverse makes Homun feel like an afterthought, when it's often the part people remember most.

💵 Practical notes

Cenotes are cash businesses across the whole route, and ATMs in small towns are unreliable — carry pesos. Only biodegradable sunscreen is allowed in the water. Water shoes are worth packing; limestone stairs get slick.
